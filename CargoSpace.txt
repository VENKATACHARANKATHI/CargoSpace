Build this as a verified freight-consolidation platform, not a marketplace that sells a container's “unused CBM.” The difficult parts are proving that cargo can actually be accepted, maintaining trustworthy capacity, preventing conflicting bookings, and coordinating the warehouse, documents, payment, and shipment.

My recommended foundation is Next.js + TypeScript, NestJS, PostgreSQL/PostGIS, Redis/BullMQ, and a separate Python optimization service, delivered first as a multilingual progressive web app.

Two boundaries matter before the specification:

• Your deadline, team, budget, and intended launch corridor are not filled in. The plan below assumes four developers, a 12-week academic/prototype build, and a subsequent controlled commercial pilot. It is not a promise of a fully operational national logistics business in 12 weeks.
• Research verification is partial. The search retrieved useful logistics-policy and container-loading sources, but did not establish reliable current Indian LCL market sizing, every competitor's capabilities, current service quotas, or every regulatory/API condition. Those items are explicitly marked for verification rather than presented as facts.

Project definition and feasibility
1.1 Working identity

Working name: CargoSpace

Descriptive title: Verified, Constraint-Aware Shared Freight Booking for Small Exporters.

Before commercial use, check trademark and domain availability. The name here is only a project label.

1.2 Refined abstract

Small exporters often depend on fragmented communication with logistics providers to identify suitable less-than-container-load capacity, compare charges, and coordinate cargo acceptance. A quoted volume alone does not establish booking feasibility because shipment suitability also depends on routing, cargo readiness, dimensional fit, weight, stackability, handling restrictions, documentation, and consolidation deadlines.

CargoSpace is a proposed digital platform connecting self-registered traders with inspection-approved logistics service providers. Providers publish time-bound consolidation capacity, while traders search by pickup location, destination, cargo characteristics, and readiness date. A matching service filters operationally incompatible options and evaluates packing feasibility when sufficiently detailed load information is available. Transactional capacity holds, payment reconciliation, role-based communication, and milestone tracking support reliable booking execution.

The platform distinguishes advertised capacity from warehouse-confirmed acceptance, records inventory freshness, and preserves an auditable history of provider approval, booking, payment, and shipment events. Its research component evaluates whether uncertainty-aware, constraint-based matching reduces infeasible booking recommendations compared with volume-only allocation.

1.3 What is realistic?

| Delivery stage | Realistic outcome |
|---|---|
| 12-week student build | Working demonstration with sandbox payments, simulated tracking, onboarding, bookings, and packing experiments |
| Controlled commercial pilot | One corridor, a few contracted LSPs, actual inspections, paid infrastructure, support and reconciliation |

An all-mode, all-cargo, nationwide marketplace with live government APIs, automated KYC, escrow, guaranteed packing, and zero operating budget is not a realistic first release.

1.5 Scope boundaries

First commercial pilot:

• One origin cluster and one consolidation corridor.
• Ambient, non-hazardous, packaged general cargo.
• Road pickup to a consolidation hub, followed by one ocean LCL service.

• Milestone tracking rather than invented end-to-end live GPS.

Out of scope initially:

• Full multimodal optimization.

The data model can accommodate road, rail, sea, and air providers, but each mode needs its own operational and pricing rules.

Domain research and market framing
2.1 How LCL really works

Less-than-container-load freight consolidates consignments from multiple shippers into a shared ocean container. Exporters generally buy a forwarding/consolidation service, not ownership of a particular geometric cavity.

A realistic journey is:

Trader submits dimensions, weight, commodity, readiness, and destination.
Provider quotes freight plus applicable origin, destination, and additional charges.
Cargo is collected or delivered to a designated facility.
The warehouse measures, weighs, and checks cargo and documentation.
The consolidator plans loading and completes operational requirements.
Cargo is stuffed, sealed, transported, and handed across the booked service.
Destination deconsolidation and delivery follow the contractual scope.

Important product correction: A physical container number may not exist when a trader books. Support a planned consolidation departure, with the container assignment added later.

The requirement to show space container-wise can therefore display:

• Confirmed container assignments.
• Planned consolidation units clearly labelled as not yet assigned.
• Provider-declared versus packing-checked capacity.
• Last inventory update and booking cutoff.

Never fabricate a container identifier to make the interface look complete.

2.2 CBM, chargeable weight, and quotations

For identical rectangular packages:

$$
V{\text{m}^3}
=
n \times L{\text{m}} \times W{\text{m}} \times H{\text{m}}
$$

If ten cartons measure 0.6 m × 0.4 m × 0.5 m, their total geometric volume is 1.2 m³.

Geometric volume is not the entire price. Ocean LCL tariffs commonly use weight-or-measure conventions, while road and air services may use different dimensional-weight factors, rounding, and minimum charges. Store these as tariff rules rather than hard-coding a universal divisor.

A configurable weight-or-measure calculation is:

$$
Q{\text{billable}}
=
\max
\left(
\frac{V}{V{\text{unit}}},
\frac{M}{M{\text{unit}}}
\right)
$$

Apply the provider's agreed minimums, rounding, unit price, and surcharge rules afterward.

Every quote should identify:

• Service scope: Pickup, CFS handling, freight, customs-related services, destination handling, or delivery.
• Basis: Dimensions and weights declared by the trader or measured by the warehouse.
• Included charges: Separately itemized.
• Excluded or conditional charges: Explicitly disclosed.
• Validity: Expiration, cutoff, and applicable departure.
• Adjustment policy: What happens if actual cargo differs from the declaration.

Do not describe a booking as all-inclusive when destination charges remain unknown.

2.3 Operational constraints

| Constraint | Required representation |
|---|---|
| Cargo-ready date | Earliest collection or acceptance time |
| Warehouse cutoff | Latest arrival for the consolidation |
| Documentation cutoff | Separate deadline from physical cargo |
| Stuffing window | Facility-specific start and end |
| Departure schedule | Planned and actual times |
| Payload | Remaining allowable cargo mass |
| Package access | Door dimensions and handling access |
| Stackability | Allowed support and top-load limits |
| Compatibility | Restricted combinations and segregations |
| Multi-stop unloading | Access order and rehandling constraints |
| Temperature | Required range and supported equipment |
| Inventory freshness | Source, timestamp, and expiry policy |

VGM is not the same as remaining payload. Keep container tare, cargo mass, packaging/dunnage where relevant, and verified gross mass records distinct. Road axle compliance requires vehicle and load-position information; a container-level weight check alone cannot establish it.

Operational and legal implementation of these requirements needs confirmation from the contracted consolidator and qualified shipping/compliance personnel.

2.4 Hub model versus milk run

Start with a hub model. Traders deliver cargo to a designated facility, or the provider arranges a separately priced pickup.

A milk run adds a collection-routing problem with:

• Pickup windows.
• Vehicle capacity.
• Travel-time uncertainty.
• Driver and vehicle availability.
• Loading order.
• Missed-pickup handling.

Treat milk-run optimization as a later module, not a prerequisite for booking shared freight.

2.5 Market size: what can be said honestly

A defensible current India-only LCL market value was not established by this research. Total logistics expenditure, total freight forwarding revenue, container throughput, and LCL platform revenue are different quantities and must not be substituted for one another.

An accessible commercial report describes the global LCL freight-forwarding segment, but its public material does not establish an independently verified Indian market size suitable for your investment case. Dataintelo

Use a bottom-up pilot model instead:

$$
\text{Annual addressable freight spend}
=
N{\text{eligible exporters}}
\times
B{\text{annual bookings}}
\times
P{\text{average booking}}
$$

$$
\text{Potential platform revenue}
=
\text{captured freight spend}
\times
\text{effective commission rate}
$$

For an illustrative assumption, not a market finding:

• 200 reachable exporters.
• 12 qualifying bookings per exporter per year.
• ₹20,000 average booking value.

That represents ₹4.8 crore annual booking value. At an illustrative 5% commission, revenue would be ₹24 lakh before support, acquisition, payment, refunds, and operating costs.

Validate every assumption through interviews and actual pilot transactions.

3.3 A defensible research direction

Proposed contribution: Uncertainty-aware, constraint-based booking admission for partially observed consolidation inventory.

The practical problem is that a platform may know declared remaining volume and weight but not the exact arrangement of existing cargo. New information can also arrive after a quote is generated.

Study whether the system can improve decisions by combining:

Operational compatibility: Route, deadlines, service scope, cargo category.
Physical feasibility: Dimensions, support, orientation, payload, known occupied regions.
Data confidence: Freshness, measurement source, inspection evidence, historical discrepancies.
Transactional admission: Holds and payment-state coordination.
Conservative handling of uncertainty: Automatic acceptance only when evidence is sufficient.

Research questions
• Does constraint-aware matching reduce infeasible recommendations relative to volume-only filtering?
• Does accounting for stale or uncertain inventory improve reliable booking completion?
• What is the trade-off between utilization, rejection rate, and decision latency?
• Does confidence-aware admission help beyond hard constraints alone?

Baselines and evaluation

| Experiment element | Proposed design |
|---|---|
| Baseline A | Remaining-volume filter |
| Baseline B | Volume, weight, route, and time constraints |
| Baseline C | Deterministic 3D packing with known geometry |
| Proposed method | Constraint-aware packing plus uncertainty-aware admission |
| Ablation 1 | Remove freshness handling |
| Ablation 2 | Remove confidence-based manual review |
| Stress tests | Stale inventory, inconsistent dimensions, concurrent checkout, missing load geometry |

Measure:

• False-feasible recommendation rate.
• False-rejection rate.
• Accepted and realized utilization.
• Computation time, especially p95.
• Booking completion and cancellation.
• Inventory conflicts.
• Review workload.
• Disparities in outcomes between new and established providers.

Do not invent experimental improvements. Synthetic datasets can establish algorithmic behavior; they cannot prove real-world business performance.

Alternative outcomes:

NEEDSCHANGES, REJECTED, SUSPENDED, REINSPECTIONDUE.

Booking:

DRAFT -> QUOTED -> HELD -> PAYMENTPENDING -> CONFIRMED -> RECEIVED -> ACCEPTEDFORLOADING -> STUFFED -> DEPARTED -> ARRIVED -> DELIVERED

5.2 Why not the obvious alternatives?

Firebase-only: Quick for authentication and basic messaging, but relational quotes, invoices, inspections, and concurrent inventory admission are more naturally expressed in PostgreSQL.

MongoDB-first: Possible, but offers little advantage for a strongly relational financial and operational workflow.

Django instead of NestJS: A strong alternative if the team is Python-heavy. Django, PostgreSQL/PostGIS, Celery, Redis, and a React frontend can implement the same architecture.

Kubernetes: Unnecessary for the pilot. It increases operational work without proving business value.

Blockchain: Not required. It does not establish whether a provider's declared physical capacity is true.

Elasticsearch: Add only after PostgreSQL search is demonstrably insufficient.

Supabase: Useful as managed PostgreSQL/auth/storage infrastructure, but not a replacement for server-side booking rules and payment reconciliation.

7.3 Database rules

Store money in integer minor units, with currency. Avoid binary floating-point arithmetic for invoices and settlements.

Use:

• Canonical integer dimensions, such as millimetres.
• Canonical integer mass units where practical.
• timestamptz for event timestamps.
• Explicit local time-zone identifiers for facility schedules.
• Unique constraints on provider payment event IDs and scoped idempotency keys.
• Check constraints on non-negative capacities, amounts, and package counts.
• Foreign keys and organization-scoped access checks.
• Version numbers for optimistic concurrency on listings and packing plans.

Store a snapshot of the accepted quote and policy, not merely a link to a mutable tariff.

7.4 Indexing

Recommended indexes:

• GiST on facility/geographic pickup points.
• B-tree on (originfacilityid, destinationfacilityid, etd).
• B-tree on (providerorgid, status, updatedat).
• B-tree on (capacitypoolid, state, expiresat) for holds.
• B-tree on (bookingid, eventtime) for tracking.
• Unique index on (gateway, providereventid).
• Unique index on (conversationid, senderid, clientmessageid).

Proximity is only candidate generation. The nearest container is not necessarily on the correct destination corridor or reachable before cutoff.

7.5 Storage and retention

| Data category | Storage approach |
|---|---|
| Active bookings and inventory | PostgreSQL |
| Chat metadata and message text | PostgreSQL initially |
| Documents and inspection images | Private object storage |
| Search results and rate-limit counters | Redis with short TTL |
| Authoritative capacity holds | PostgreSQL, with Redis only as an acceleration layer |
| Large event history | Partitioned PostgreSQL when volume warrants it |
| Archived documents/events | Lower-cost object storage under retention policy |
| Logs | Centralized, redacted, bounded retention |

Backups need encrypted database backups, object-version protection where appropriate, and tested restoration. A backup that has never been restored is not a demonstrated recovery capability.

Matching, packing, and DAA design
8.1 Why free CBM is insufficient

Two containers can each have 5 m³ unoccupied volume but accept completely different cargo.

A load can fail because:

• The package cannot pass through the door.
• Empty space is fragmented.
• The remaining floor space is unsuitable.
• Existing cargo cannot support the load.
• Weight or load-distribution constraints would be exceeded.
• The intended unloading order becomes impossible.

The optimizer cannot infer exact geometry from total remaining CBM.

Use three evidence levels:

| Level | Available data | Permitted outcome |
|---|---|---|
| Declared | Remaining volume and mass only | Conditional quote or manual acceptance |
| Structured | Package dimensions and occupancy approximation | Conservative feasibility estimate |
| Measured | Trusted placement/occupancy and handling constraints | Detailed packing evaluation, still subject to operational checks |

8.2 Matching pipeline
Filter to approved providers and supported cargo types.
Match destination corridor and service scope.
Check readiness, pickup feasibility, and cutoffs.
Exclude stale or suspended inventory.
Apply volume and mass bounds.
Apply compatibility and handling constraints.
Evaluate geometry where sufficient evidence exists.
Rank feasible options.
Revalidate at checkout under database concurrency control.

A ranking function may be:

$$
Sj =
wp \widehat{Pj}
• wt \widehat{Tj}
• wd \widehat{Dj}
• wu \widehat{Uj}
• wr \widehat{Rj}
$$

Here, the normalized terms represent price, time, pickup distance, uncertainty, and operational risk. Lower is preferable.

Hard safety and compatibility constraints are filters, not score penalties. A low price must never compensate for prohibited cargo.

8.3 Packing strategy

Begin with an extreme-point or maximal-empty-space heuristic:

• Sort packages by a declared rule, such as descending volume.
• Enumerate permitted orientations.
• Generate candidate placement points.
• Reject overlap, boundary, support, and payload violations.
• Score feasible placements.
• Use bounded search or local improvement where required.

For small instances, a constraint-programming or mathematical-programming model can provide comparison solutions. Do not promise exact optimal packing at large scale.

Existing physical cargo may be fixed in place. Distinguish “repacking permitted” from “existing placements immutable.”

8.4 Concurrency and geometry

Scalar capacity counters alone cannot prevent two bookings from claiming the same geometric space.

Use:

Read inventory and packing-plan version.
Compute candidate placement outside the database lock.
Begin transaction and lock the relevant pool.
Verify the inventory and plan versions have not changed.
Commit both the resource hold and placement reservation.
Retry or downgrade to manual review if versions changed.

This keeps expensive optimization outside long-held transactions while preserving correctness at commit time.

8.5 Checkout hold policy

A 10-minute hold is a configurable product starting point, not a legal or industry requirement.

The expiry worker should not be the only enforcement mechanism. Every booking decision must evaluate expiresat using authoritative server/database time, so a delayed worker cannot leave expired reservations effectively active.

External double-selling remains possible when an LSP also sells through phone calls or other systems. Address it through inventory integration, conservative buffers, provider obligations, and warehouse confirmation.

Trust, identities, cargo, money, and documents
9.1 Provider trust model

Approval should cover:

• Legal organization and authorized representative.
• Facilities and operational access.
• Ability to offer the listed services.
• Safety and handling procedures.
• Accuracy of sample schedules and capacity.
• Claims and incident handling.
• Contractual rights to sell the offered capacity.

An internal data-quality score can assess completeness, timeliness, and observed discrepancies. It should not substitute for inspection or become an unexplained public “safety score.”

Triggers for reinspection include expired evidence, facility changes, serious complaints, repeated capacity discrepancies, or a material service-scope change.

9.2 Indian identity verification

| Identity/document | Purpose | Implementation boundary |
|---|---|---|
| IEC | Import/export business identity where applicable | Capture and verify through an authorized workflow; open free production API not established |
| GSTIN | Tax registration identity | Syntax validation is not registration verification |
| CIN | Company registration reference where applicable | Not every trader is a company |
| Aadhaar | Sensitive individual identity | Do not make it the default business onboarding requirement |

9.3 Cargo admission

For the MVP, explicitly reject unsupported dangerous goods, perishables, reefer cargo, prohibited items, and regulated categories.

Before expanding:

• Obtain mode-specific dangerous-goods expertise.
• Implement classification, packaging, documentation, and segregation rules.
• Confirm equipment and facility capability.
• Define incident handling.
• Contract for responsibility and liability.
• Distinguish insurance coverage from a provider's limited contractual liability.

A checkbox saying “not dangerous” is useful disclosure, but not a complete dangerous-goods compliance system.

9.4 Money flow

A database capacity hold is not a card authorization hold, and neither is escrow.

Recommended flow:

Trader accepts a versioned quote.
Platform reserves capacity.
Trader pays through a hosted gateway checkout.
Backend verifies payment events and reconciles exceptions.
Provider settlement follows the contracted, gateway-approved model.
Refunds and disputes are independently tracked.

Use a licensed payment partner's permitted marketplace/split-settlement product, subject to its approval. Do not receive customer funds in a personal or ordinary account and market that as escrow.

Payment architecture, GST, invoicing, and liability require a qualified lawyer/accountant and the payment provider's review; this is a technical design, not licensed legal or financial advice.

9.5 Cancellation and no-show policy

Publish rules for:

• Trader cancellation before cargo handover.
• Cancellation after pickup or warehouse handling.
• Missed cargo/document cutoff.
• Provider cancellation.
• Changed measured dimensions or weight.
• Vessel/route changes.
• Prohibited or misdeclared cargo.
• Failed collection.
• Refund processing and deductions.

The policy accepted at booking must remain recoverable, even if the public policy later changes.

IEEE Access-oriented paper draft

This is a research manuscript draft, not a completed study. It deliberately contains no fabricated results, author affiliations, ethics approvals, or claims of acceptance. Current IEEE Access formatting and submission requirements must be verified before submission.

Title

Uncertainty-Aware Capacity Matching and Transactional Booking for Shared Freight Consolidation

Abstract

Digital freight platforms can improve access to shared transportation capacity, but advertised residual volume does not necessarily imply operationally feasible cargo acceptance. Booking decisions depend on routing, readiness deadlines, package geometry, payload, handling restrictions, and the freshness of provider-reported inventory. This paper proposes an architecture for shared freight consolidation that combines inspection-gated provider onboarding, constraint-aware matching, transactional capacity reservation, and auditable booking execution. The proposed admission process distinguishes declared inventory from structured and measured load information, applying conservative or manual acceptance when evidence is insufficient. A separate optimization service evaluates package placement where adequate geometric information is available, while a transactional booking service coordinates inventory holds and asynchronous payment events. The evaluation protocol compares volume-only, deterministic constraint-based, and uncertainty-aware approaches under changing inventory and concurrent demand. The intended contribution is a reproducible framework for studying reliable booking admission under partially observed freight capacity. Empirical performance and operational validity remain to be established through controlled experiments and a provider-supported pilot.

Index terms: Freight consolidation, container loading, logistics platforms, uncertainty-aware matching, transactional reservation, geospatial search.

I. Introduction

Small consignments are commonly consolidated to share transport capacity. Digital discovery can reduce communication effort, but reliable booking requires more than exposing a provider's declared remaining volume. Physical fit, handling compatibility, readiness, facility deadlines, and inventory updates influence whether a consignment can actually be accepted.

Existing container-loading research establishes the importance of practical constraints. However, packing feasibility and marketplace booking are often treated as separate concerns. A feasible plan may become obsolete before payment completes, while a successful payment does not prove that physical capacity remains available. RePEc

This work proposes a system that connects constrained matching with evidence-aware admission and transactional reservations. The intended contributions are:

A representation of freight capacity that includes operational deadlines and data confidence.
An admission workflow distinguishing verified feasibility from conditional acceptance.
A concurrency-safe architecture connecting packing-plan versions, capacity holds, and payment events.
An evaluation protocol covering feasibility errors, utilization, latency, and inventory staleness.

III. Problem Formulation

Let the set of shipments be $$I$$ and the set of available consolidation departures be $$J$$. Each shipment has package geometry, mass, origin, destination, readiness, and handling requirements. Each departure has capacity, cutoffs, service compatibility, inventory age, and a known or partially known loading configuration.

Define binary assignment variable $$x{ij}$$ to indicate whether shipment $$i$$ is allocated to departure $$j$$.

A representative objective is:

$$
\min
\sum{i \in I}
\sum{j \in J}
x{ij}
\left(
\alpha C{ij}
• \beta T{ij}
• \gamma U{ij}
\right)
• \lambda
\sum{i \in I} yi
$$

Here, cost, timing, and uncertainty penalties are balanced against an unassigned-shipment penalty.

$$
\sum{j \in J} x{ij} + y_i = 1
\qquad \forall i \in I
$$

Assignments must satisfy route compatibility, time windows, supported cargo types, capacity limits, and applicable loading constraints.

Aggregate volume and mass inequalities are necessary screening conditions, not sufficient packing conditions. Detailed geometry requires non-overlap, boundary, orientation, and support constraints.

The method should not describe an uncertainty score as a calibrated probability unless calibration is actually measured.

IV. Proposed Method

The proposed pipeline first filters departures using service compatibility and operational deadlines. It then applies aggregate capacity checks and invokes geometric evaluation where data quality permits.

Inventory is classified by evidence level. Declared-only inventory can produce conditional recommendations, while sufficiently measured inventory supports detailed feasibility analysis. The final admission decision is committed only after verifying the current inventory and packing-plan versions.

A capacity hold reserves the selected resources for a bounded interval. Payment processing uses verified asynchronous events and idempotent financial records. Expired holds and delayed payment success are handled as explicit exceptions rather than unconditional confirmation.

V. System Implementation

The proposed implementation uses a TypeScript business API, PostgreSQL/PostGIS for transactional and geographic data, and a Python optimization service. Background workers process outbox events, notifications, reconciliation, and inventory imports. Private object storage contains operational documents and inspection evidence.

Role-based and organization-scoped authorization separates traders, providers, inspectors, and administrators. Provider approval is scoped to inspected services and facilities. Shipment events retain both event time and receipt time to support delayed updates.

VI. Experimental Design

Evaluate four configurations:

• Volume-only screening.
• Volume, payload, route, and time screening.
• Deterministic geometric packing.
• Constraint-aware matching with uncertainty-aware admission.

Generate datasets varying package dimensions, occupancy fragmentation, cargo mix, cutoff proximity, stale inventory, and concurrent bookings. Document seeds, distributions, hardware, time limits, and solver versions.

For small instances, compare feasibility against an exact or independently validated method where practical. For operational claims, obtain measured warehouse data or supervised pilot outcomes.

Report feasibility precision and recall, utilization, latency, booking conflicts, review workload, and sensitivity to uncertainty parameters. Use repeated trials and uncertainty intervals appropriate to the experimental design.

VII. Results

Results are pending implementation and evaluation.

The final manuscript should present measured comparisons, ablations, failure cases, and computational costs. It should not infer reduced logistics costs or emissions from improved simulated packing alone.

VIII. Limitations and Threats to Validity

Synthetic package distributions may not represent actual cargo. Provider reporting behavior can differ from simulated staleness. Loading success depends on factors that software may not fully observe, including packaging strength, handling practices, and measurement errors.

Manual acceptance introduces operator variability. Historical provider reliability may disadvantage new providers if used without safeguards. A single-corridor pilot limits generalizability across modes, commodities, and regions.

IX. Conclusion

The proposed system treats shared-freight booking as a combination of operational matching, physical feasibility, evidence quality, and transactional consistency. Its research value depends on demonstrating that uncertainty-aware admission improves meaningful outcomes beyond deterministic constraints alone. The architecture provides a basis for implementation and controlled evaluation, but commercial effectiveness and empirical improvements remain unproven.

Final recommendation

Build the reliable booking platform first; make the optimizer a measurable research module, not a marketing claim.

The strongest project submission will demonstrate:

A genuine inspection-to-approval workflow.
Cargo-aware, cutoff-aware search.
Correct inventory holds under concurrency.
Safe payment and refund exception handling.
Clear limits when geometric data is incomplete.
A reproducible experiment against honest baselines.
A narrow pilot supported by real logistics operators.

The main unresolved diligence items are current provider/API quotas, authorized identity-verification access, applicable legal commencement dates, a verified competitor feature audit, and actual corridor economics. Resolve those before committing to production providers or claiming market readiness.
